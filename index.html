Chapter one

What makes a chronograph a “flyback”?

A chronograph is a watch with a built-in stopwatch. On a standard chronograph, timing a new event takes three presses: stop, reset, then start again. That's slow if you're timing several things back to back.

A flyback chronograph removes the delay. While the stopwatch is running, one press of the reset pusher sends the hand “flying back” to zero and it immediately starts timing again. It was developed in the 1930s with pilots in mind, who needed to time consecutive legs of a flight without fumbling with three buttons.

    Chapter two

    The three kinds of watch movement

    The movement — sometimes called the calibre — is the engine of a watch. Each type has its own character, strengths and care needs.

    An automatic movement winds itself. A weighted rotor spins as your wrist moves and tightens the mainspring. Wear it daily and it keeps running; leave it in a drawer and it will stop after its power reserve runs out, often around 38–80 hours.

    Great forEveryday wear with the charm of a mechanical movement and a smoothly sweeping seconds hand.
    Keep in mindAccuracy of a few seconds a day is normal. Plan a service every 5–10 years.

    A manual-wind movement must be wound by hand using the crown, usually once a day. With no rotor, these movements are often thinner and let you see more of the mechanism through a display caseback.

    Great forSlim dress watches and anyone who enjoys a small daily ritual with their watch.
    Keep in mindWind gently until you feel resistance, then stop — never force the crown.

    A quartz movement is powered by a battery. An electric current makes a tiny quartz crystal vibrate at 32,768 times per second, and a circuit turns those vibrations into precise timekeeping.

    Great forGrab-and-go reliability and accuracy, often within a few seconds a month.
    Keep in mindReplace the battery every 2–5 years and have seals checked at the same time.
    Chapter three

    Why bronze watches change color — and why people love it

    Bronze is an alloy made mostly of copper, typically with tin and sometimes aluminum. When it meets air, moisture and the oils on your skin, the surface slowly oxidizes and forms a patina. No two bronze watches age the same way: your climate, habits and even how often you swim all shape the result.

    Many owners enjoy the patina as a record of where the watch has been. Others prefer it bright. Both are fine — and the patina can be gently removed if you change your mind.

    Weeks 0–2

    Fresh

    Bright, rosy-gold metal straight from the box, similar in tone to a new coin.

    Weeks 2–8

    Warming

    The shine softens to amber and honey tones, first on edges and the caseback.

    Months

    Mature

    Deep brown, even coloring develops, with character around the lugs and crown.

    Humid or salty conditions

    Verdigris

    Green-blue spots may appear. Wipe them away with a soft cloth if you prefer.

    Want to brighten a bronze case? Gently rub with a soft cloth and a small amount of mild toothpaste, then wipe clean. Keep cleaning products away from the crown, pushers and caseback seals, and remove leather straps first.

    Which case size suits your wrist?

    Case diameter gets the attention, but lug-to-lug length — the distance from the top lug tip to the bottom one — decides whether a watch actually fits. Measure your wrist with a soft tape just below the wrist bone.

    Chapter five

    What does “water resistant to 100 m” really mean?

    Water-resistance ratings describe the pressure a watch withstood in a laboratory test — not a depth you can safely dive to. Real life adds movement, temperature changes and ageing gaskets, so it's wise to treat ratings conservatively.

    Choose your watch's rating to see which everyday activities it's generally suited to. And remember: water resistance isn't permanent. Seals dry out, so have them checked every year or two if you swim regularly.

    Chapter six

    Choosing a strap that changes the whole watch

    A new strap is the easiest way to give a watch a new personality. Start by measuring the lug width — the gap between the lugs — in millimeters; most watches use 18, 20 or 22 mm.

    Smooth leather

    Polished and classic. Lovely with dress watches, but keep it away from water and sweat.

    Best for: office, evenings

    Suede & distressed leather

    Soft and relaxed, with a texture that pairs beautifully with bronze and vintage-style cases.

    Best for: weekends, autumn

    Rubber & fabric

    Water-friendly, light and easy to clean. The practical choice for swimming, travel and summer.

    Best for: water, sport, heat

    Metal bracelet

    Durable and versatile, though a little heavier. Ask a jeweler to adjust links for a comfortable fit.

    Best for: daily wear, all seasons
    Chapter seven

    Eight habits that keep a watch healthy for decades

    A mechanical watch is built to last generations. These simple habits — no tools required — make the biggest difference between services.

    1. Set the date away from midnight

      Avoid changing the date between roughly 9 pm and 3 am, when the date mechanism may be engaged.

    2. Keep the crown pushed in

      A pulled-out or unscrewed crown is the most common way water gets inside.

    3. Keep away from strong magnets

      Speakers, tablet covers and bag clasps can magnetize a movement and make it run fast.

    4. Avoid hot water and saunas

      Heat makes gaskets expand and contract, weakening water resistance over time.

    5. Rinse after salt or chlorine

      A quick rinse in fresh water, with the crown closed, stops residue building up.

    6. Wind gently and regularly

      For manual watches, wind at about the same time each day until you feel resistance.

    7. Store it thoughtfully

      Keep watches apart in a soft box or roll so crystals and cases don't scratch each other.

    8. Service on schedule

      Most mechanical watches benefit from a full service every 5–10 years; follow your maker's guidance.

    Reference

    Watch words, decoded

    Search for any term you come across in a spec sheet or listing.

    Bezel

    The ring around the crystal. Some rotate to track elapsed time or a second time zone.

    Calibre

    A movement's model name or number, used to identify its design.

    Chronograph

    A watch with a stopwatch function, usually controlled by pushers.

    Complication

    Any function beyond hours, minutes and seconds — a date, chronograph or moon phase.

    Crown

    The knob used to set the time and, on many watches, wind the mainspring.

    Crystal

    The transparent cover over the dial, commonly sapphire, mineral glass or acrylic.

    Escapement

    The mechanism that releases the movement's energy in small, regular steps.

    GMT

    A complication that shows a second time zone, often with a 24-hour hand.

    Jewels

    Synthetic ruby bearings that reduce friction at key points in a movement.

    Lug-to-lug

    The length of the case from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom lugs.

    Mainspring

    The coiled spring that stores the energy that powers a mechanical watch.

    Patina

    The natural color change on bronze and some dials as materials age.

    Power reserve

    How long a fully wound watch runs before it stops.

    Rotor

    The weighted, spinning part that winds an automatic movement.

    Tachymeter

    A scale for calculating speed over a known distance using the chronograph.

    Questions

    Frequently asked questions

    Is it normal for a mechanical watch to gain or lose time?

    Yes. Mechanical watches are affected by position, temperature and how fully wound they are. Gaining or losing a few seconds a day is typical; if yours drifts by a minute or more, it may be magnetized or due for a service.

    Will bronze turn my wrist green?

    Most bronze watches use a steel or titanium caseback so the metal touching your skin doesn't react. If your caseback is bronze, any green mark is harmless and washes off with soap and water.

    Can I leave an automatic watch in a drawer?

    Yes. It will simply stop when the power reserve runs out. Before wearing it again, give the crown a few gentle turns to build power, then set the time and date.

    Do I need a watch winder?

    No. A winder is a convenience, not a necessity. Letting a watch stop between wears doesn't harm it.
